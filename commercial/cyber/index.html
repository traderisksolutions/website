The cover

Cyber insurance in Singapore

Cyber insurance pays the costs of responding to a network intrusion, ransomware event or data breach, and the liability that follows. First-party costs include forensics, legal advice, notification, data restoration, extortion payments where lawful and lost profit during an outage. Third-party cover answers claims by customers and regulatory investigations under the PDPA. Singapore buyers span tech, finance, logistics, healthcare, retail and professional services. Large customers and government bodies now require vendors to hold it, and boards ask for it after the CSA recorded 165 ransomware cases in 2025.

It is not cover for upgrading security after an incident, nor for losses the business could have prevented with the controls it declared on the proposal form. Liability sections are claims-made with a retroactive date; first-party sections respond to events discovered during the period. The buyer sets an aggregate limit, sub-limits for extortion and business interruption, a waiting period for outages and a deductible. Insurers require multifactor authentication, offline backups and patch management before quoting. The broker checks the controls, completes the questionnaire and runs the incident response panel.

Who buys it

  • Any organisation holding personal data of 500 or more individuals, where a breach must be reported to the PDPC.
  • Vendors to banks, government agencies and multinationals whose contracts require cyber cover.
  • E-commerce, SaaS and fintech firms whose revenue stops when systems go down.
  • Clinics and healthcare groups holding patient records.
  • Manufacturers and logistics operators exposed to ransomware on operational systems.
  • Professional firms whose client files and payment instructions attract fraud.

What the insurer needs to quote

  • Annual turnover and number of personal data records held
  • Completed insurer security questionnaire or self-assessment
  • Evidence of multifactor authentication, backups and patching cadence
  • Incident, breach and PDPC notification history for five years
  • List of critical IT vendors and cloud providers
  • Contracts that require a stated cyber limit

Price and terms

What moves the premium

Revenue and records
Insurers rate on turnover and the number of personal data records held; both set the size of a breach.
Controls
Multifactor authentication, offline backups, endpoint detection and patch timelines decide whether a quote is offered and at what rate.
Sector
Healthcare, finance and critical infrastructure rate higher because records are sensitive and downtime is costly.
Limit and sub-limits
The aggregate limit plus sub-limits for extortion, business interruption and social engineering drive the premium.
Waiting period
A longer waiting period before business interruption cover starts lowers the premium.
Incident history
Prior ransomware, breaches or PDPC findings are priced in; an undeclared incident voids the policy.

At renewal

Three mistakes we see most often

Security answers do not match reality
The proposal says MFA is enforced everywhere and the breach comes through an unprotected VPN account, so the insurer declines for misrepresentation. Have IT verify every control answer and keep the evidence before signing.
PDPC deadline missed
The breach is found, the IT team spends weeks on recovery, and no one assesses whether it is notifiable, so the organisation breaches the Data Breach Notification Obligation on top of the breach itself. Assess within 30 calendar days of discovery and notify the PDPC within three calendar days of deciding it is notifiable.
Vendor outage not covered
A cloud provider or payment processor goes down, the business loses a week of sales, and the policy covers only outages of its own network. Buy contingent business interruption with the key vendors named.

Questions

Questions buyers ask

When must a data breach be reported to the PDPC?

A breach is notifiable if it results in, or is likely to result in, significant harm to affected individuals, or if it affects 500 or more individuals. The organisation must assess within 30 calendar days of having credible grounds to believe a breach occurred, and notify the PDPC as soon as practicable and within three calendar days of determining it is notifiable. Affected individuals are told at the same time or after.

How much can the PDPC fine?

From 1 October 2022 the maximum financial penalty for breaching the data protection provisions is 10% of annual Singapore turnover for organisations with turnover above S$10 million, and S$1 million for all others. In October 2025 the PDPC fined Marina Bay Sands S$315,000 for a 2023 breach affecting more than 600,000 patrons. Whether a fine is insurable depends on the wording and the law.

Does cyber insurance pay a ransom?

Most policies include cyber extortion cover that reimburses a ransom payment and the negotiator's fees, subject to a sub-limit and the insurer's prior consent. Payment must be lawful; sanctions screening is done before any transfer. Insurers prefer restoration from offline backups, which is why tested backups are a condition of cover and a ransom is the last resort.

What security controls do insurers require before quoting?

The baseline in the Singapore market is multifactor authentication on remote access and email, offline or disconnected backups tested for restoration, and formal patch management with faster fixes for high and critical vulnerabilities. Larger firms are asked for endpoint detection and response, privileged access management and an incident response plan. Weaker controls mean a higher premium, lower sub-limits or a declined quote.

Is business interruption covered?

Yes, for net profit lost and extra expense incurred while systems are down after a covered event, once a waiting period has passed. Cover for outages at a cloud or technology vendor is a separate extension, usually sub-limited and sometimes restricted to named providers. Loss of revenue from reputational harm after systems are restored is a further extension on some wordings.

Does the policy cover payment fraud by email?

Only under a social engineering or funds transfer fraud extension, which is often sub-limited well below the main limit. The loss is money the business was tricked into paying, not data. In November 2025 a Singapore logistics firm received a payment-redirection email for US$18,288; that is the shape of the claim. Dual authorisation on bank detail changes is usually a condition of the extension.
