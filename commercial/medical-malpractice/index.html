The cover

Medical malpractice insurance in Singapore

Medical malpractice insurance pays the damages and defence costs a doctor, dentist, allied health practitioner or clinic owes when a patient alleges negligent diagnosis, treatment or advice. It also funds legal representation before the Singapore Medical Council and at coroner's inquests. No Singapore statute makes it compulsory, but private hospitals require proof of cover before granting practising privileges, and doctors in public institutions have been covered by the MOH Holdings programme since 2017. Clinic licensees under the Healthcare Services Act buy entity cover for staff and locums.

It is not cover for fines imposed for non-clinical breaches, nor for criminal acts. It is claims-made: the policy must be in force when the claim is made and reported, and a retroactive date sets how far back the treatment can go. The buyer chooses a limit per claim and per year, a deductible and a territory. The broker checks run-off terms for retirement, compares the discretionary cover of a defence organisation with a contractual policy, and arranges entity cover for the practice.

Who buys it

  • Private specialists and GPs who must show cover for hospital practising privileges.
  • Dental practices, aesthetic clinics and day surgery centres licensed under the HCSA.
  • Allied health professionals, TCM practitioners and physiotherapists in private practice.
  • Group practices and medical centres employing doctors, nurses and locums.
  • Telemedicine providers consulting patients outside Singapore.
  • Nursing homes and long-term care operators with clinical staff.

What the insurer needs to quote

  • SMC, SDC or AHPC registration and specialty
  • Hours worked, patient numbers and procedures per year
  • Hospitals and clinics where the practitioner holds privileges
  • Current cover, retroactive date and limit, or defence organisation membership
  • Claims, complaints and SMC matters for the last ten years
  • Telemedicine, overseas and locum work undertaken

Price and terms

What moves the premium

Specialty
Obstetrics, neurosurgery, spinal and aesthetic work are rated above general practice because awards are larger.
Limit
Higher limits for procedural specialties follow award sizes; the largest reported Singapore award is S$5.3 million against two treating doctors.
Patient volume and hours
Insurers rate on patients seen, procedures performed and hours worked, including locum sessions.
Claims and SMC history
Prior claims, complaints and SMC findings raise the rate; a single claim is not an automatic decline.
Territory
Telemedicine and medical tourism patients bring overseas jurisdictions; USA and Canada are excluded or priced separately.
Entity cover
Adding the clinic as an insured for staff and locums adds premium but closes the vicarious liability gap.

At renewal

Three mistakes we see most often

Switching from a discretionary scheme without run-off
A doctor leaves a mutual defence organisation for an insured policy and loses assistance on incidents from the membership years, because the old scheme was occurrence-based and the new policy is claims-made from inception. Confirm in writing what the old scheme will still assist with, and match the retroactive date to the gap.
Retirement with no run-off
The practitioner retires, stops paying, and a claim arrives four years later for a procedure done in the last year of practice. Buy a policy with automatic run-off on retirement; the claim period runs three years from when the patient first had knowledge, and for a child the clock starts at 21.
Clinic entity uninsured
Each doctor holds a personal policy but the clinic company, which the patient sues as employer of the nurse or locum, has none. Add the practice entity as an insured with its own vicarious liability cover.

Questions

Questions buyers ask

Is malpractice insurance compulsory for doctors in Singapore?

No statute requires it and the 2016 SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines do not mandate it. In practice it is unavoidable: private hospitals require evidence of cover before granting practising privileges, and public healthcare institutions cover their doctors under the MOH Holdings programme administered by Marsh. A doctor without cover carries the full defence cost and any award personally.

How long after treatment can a patient claim?

Under section 24A of the Limitation Act, a personal injury action must be brought within three years of the cause of action, or three years from the date the patient first had the knowledge needed to sue, whichever is later. Under section 24, a minor has three years from turning 21, so a claim for treatment of an infant can arrive 24 years later. That is why run-off cover matters.

Does it cover SMC disciplinary inquiries and fines?

Legal representation at SMC complaints committee and disciplinary tribunal hearings is covered by most policies, as are coroner's inquests. Fines are different. A disciplinary tribunal can fine up to S$100,000 under section 53 of the Medical Registration Act, and most policies exclude fines and penalties. A few insurers cover fines that arise from a malpractice event. Read the fines clause.

What is the difference between a defence organisation and an insurance policy?

A mutual defence organisation offers discretionary assistance under its articles; it decides case by case whether and how much to help. An insurance policy is a contract enforceable in court with a stated limit and defined exclusions. Defence organisation cover is usually occurrence-based; insurance is claims-made. Moving between them needs the retroactive date and the old scheme's run-off position confirmed in writing.

What limit should a specialist carry?

Match the limit to the worst plausible award in the specialty, not to the premium. The largest reported Singapore malpractice award is S$5.3 million against two treating doctors, and lifelong care costs rise with medical inflation. Hospitals may set a minimum in their credentialing terms. Check whether defence costs sit inside the limit and whether the limit is per claim or per year.

Does the policy cover telemedicine and overseas patients?

Only if the territory and jurisdiction clauses say so. Telemedicine is regulated under the Healthcare Services Act, and a consultation with a patient abroad can attract that country's courts. Many policies cover treatment worldwide with claims brought anywhere except USA and Canada. Declare overseas consulting, medical tourism patients and humanitarian work, which some wordings cap by days per year.

Sources: MOH: Medical Registration (Amendment) Bill, fines up to S$100,000, Limitation Act 1959, sections 24 and 24A, SMC Ethical Code and Ethical Guidelines 2016, MOH Holdings Medical Malpractice Insurance programme
