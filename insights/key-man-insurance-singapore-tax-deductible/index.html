Key man insurance in Singapore: the 5 IRAS deductibility conditions

By the TRS broking team10 Nov 20266 min read
The short answerKey man insurance in Singapore pays a company a lump sum if a named person dies or becomes disabled. IRAS allows the premium as a tax deduction only when all 5 conditions in its e-Tax Guide are met. Where the premium is deductible, the payout is taxed as a trading receipt. Where the keyman owns most of the company, the deduction is usually refused.

What key man insurance in Singapore covers

A key person policy is a life or disability policy owned by the company. The company pays the premium and receives the benefit. The insured person is an employee or director whose work drives the profit. The policy replaces the profit the business loses while it recruits, trains or re-wins customers.

The cover is term life, critical illness or total permanent disability, written for a fixed period. There is no savings element. The sum assured is set against the profit the person brings in, not against their salary or the value of their shares.

Brokers place key person insurance alongside directors' and officers' cover in most technology-company programmes. Lockton lists it as one of 8 standard covers for technology firms, next to professional indemnity, D&O and cyber. For a Singapore SME with 10 to 200 staff, the key people are usually the founder who sells, the technical lead and the finance head.

The 5 IRAS deductibility conditions

IRAS published the third edition of its e-Tax Guide, Deductibility of "Keyman" Insurance Premiums, on 30 January 2026. The starting point is Section 14 and Section 15 of the Income Tax Act 1947. A premium paid to acquire an asset for the company is capital and is not deductible. Key man insurance is the exception, and only when every condition below holds.

ConditionWhat IRAS requires
1. PurposeThe policy insures the business against loss of profits from the death or disability of the keyman
2. Sum assuredThe capital sum is directly related to the annual profits attributable to the keyman
3. OwnershipThe policy stays the company's property, with no assignment of benefits to the keyman or family
4. No investment valueThe policy has no cash surrender value and no investment value
5. Business survivesThe loss of the keyman does not destroy the entire profit-making structure

Condition 2 is the one companies most often fail. If the sum assured exceeds the annual profit of the business, IRAS treats the premium as not wholly and exclusively incurred in producing income. Condition 4 removes whole-life, endowment and investment-linked policies from the deduction, whatever label the insurer puts on them.

Who is refused the deduction?

IRAS denies the deduction where the benefit of the policy accrues to the keyman personally rather than to the business. The e-Tax Guide names 2 situations. The first is a sole proprietor insuring their own life. The business is not a separate legal entity, so the proprietor's death ends the whole profit-making structure. The second is a keyman who owns a substantial share of the company, alone or with relatives.

IRAS reached the same result in its industry write-up on family-owned companies. Some companies claimed deductions for life policies on directors that had been assigned to a bank as collateral for trade facilities. The bank was the beneficiary. IRAS refused those claims because the policies did not meet the conditions in the e-Tax Guide.

The test is applied on the facts of each case. A founder with 40% of the shares and a brother holding another 20% is likely to fail Condition 3. A hired managing director with no shareholding is likely to pass. A sole proprietor can still deduct a policy on an employee, as long as the other 4 conditions hold.

How the payout is taxed

The tax treatment of the claim follows the treatment of the premium. Where the premiums qualified for deduction, any recovery under the policy is a trading receipt and is taxed as income of the business. Where the premiums were not deductible, the payout is a capital receipt and is not taxed.

This cuts both ways in planning. A company that deducts the premium each year pays corporate tax on the sum assured when a claim arrives. A company that forgoes the deduction keeps the full payout. For a policy with a low premium and a large sum assured, the second route can leave more cash in the company after a loss. The broker and the tax adviser should model both before the policy is written.

A worked example

Example only. A design consultancy makes S$900,000 profit a year. Its creative director is responsible for the accounts that produce S$600,000 of that profit. The company holds a 5-year term policy on her life with a sum assured of S$600,000 and an annual premium of S$4,200.

  • Condition 1: the stated purpose is loss of profits. Met.
  • Condition 2: S$600,000 matches the profit attributable to her. Met.
  • Condition 3: the company owns the policy and has not assigned it. Met.
  • Condition 4: term cover, no surrender value. Met.
  • Condition 5: the firm would lose profit but could continue trading. Met.

The S$4,200 premium is deductible. If she dies in year 3, the S$600,000 payout is a trading receipt and is taxed. Had the company bought a S$2 million policy, Condition 2 would fail and the whole premium would be non-deductible, not only the excess.

What to state in the tax computation

IRAS does not ask for documents with the return. The company must state in its tax computation the basis for treating the insured person as a keyman. The e-Tax Guide defines a keyman as someone with special qualifications or experience of irreplaceable value, who has prime responsibility for bringing in profit.

The company should keep, and produce on request:

  • The policy schedule showing the company as owner and beneficiary.
  • A note linking the sum assured to the profit the keyman generates.
  • Board minutes recording the purpose of the policy.
  • The premium invoices for each year claimed.

A broker arranges the policy on the right legal basis at inception. Changing the owner or beneficiary later usually breaks Condition 3 for every year before the change. The broker's review of the employee benefits programme should include the key person policies and their sums assured each year.

Frequently asked questions

Is key man insurance tax deductible in Singapore?

Yes, when all 5 conditions in the IRAS e-Tax Guide are met. The policy must insure loss of profits and the sum assured must relate to the keyman's profit contribution. The company must own it, it must have no investment value, and the business must be able to continue without the keyman. Failing any one condition makes the whole premium non-deductible.

Can a whole-life policy qualify?

No. Condition 4 excludes any policy with a cash surrender value or investment value. IRAS applies this to life, endowment and group personal policies alike. Only pure protection cover, such as term life or disability, can be deducted.

Is the payout taxed?

The payout is taxed as a trading receipt when the premiums were deductible. When the premiums were not deductible, the payout is a capital receipt and is not taxed. The company should decide which route it wants before the policy starts.

Can I insure a co-founder who owns 40%?

The company can insure them, but the deduction is at risk. IRAS refuses the deduction where the keyman owns a substantial share alone or with relatives, because the benefit then accrues to the keyman personally. A 40% holding, with or without family shares, is likely to be treated as substantial. The policy still works as protection; only the tax treatment changes.
