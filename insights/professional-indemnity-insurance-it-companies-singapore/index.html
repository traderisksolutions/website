Professional indemnity insurance for IT companies in Singapore

By the TRS broking team10 Nov 20268 min read
The short answerProfessional indemnity for IT companies in Singapore covers the firm's legal liability when its software, advice or services cause a client financial loss. In the technology sector the policy is often called technology errors and omissions. It responds to a failed implementation, a defective release or a missed specification. It does not respond to a data breach of the firm's own systems; that is cyber insurance.

What professional indemnity covers for a technology firm

Professional indemnity (PI) insures civil liability for breach of professional duty. For an IT company the duty is to deliver work that meets the contract and the standard of a competent provider. The policy pays damages awarded or agreed, plus defence costs, up to the limit.

Technology errors and omissions wordings extend the traditional PI form in 3 ways.

  • Products and services together. A software firm sells a product and the service of implementing it. Tech E&O covers both, so a dispute about whether the fault was in the code or the configuration does not fall between two policies.
  • Breach of contract. Standard PI covers negligence. Tech E&O usually covers liability for failure to perform the contract, which is how most technology claims are pleaded.
  • Intellectual property. Unintentional infringement of copyright or trade mark in delivered work is often included. Patent infringement is usually excluded.

Typical claims are a payroll system that calculated CPF wrongly for 8 months, an e-commerce platform that went live and lost orders over a launch weekend, a migration that corrupted a client's customer database, and a consultant's architecture recommendation that the client followed and had to reverse. In each case the client's loss is financial, not physical, and the client's remedy is against the firm.

The policy excludes known circumstances, deliberate acts, fines and penalties in most forms, and liability assumed under contract beyond what the law would impose. It also excludes bodily injury and property damage, which belong to public liability. The public and product liability comparison covers that boundary.

Tech E&O vs cyber: which policy responds

The two policies are bought together and confused often. The trigger is different.

The overlap sits in the breach-of-client-data row. Insurers manage it with an other insurance clause or by placing both policies with one carrier. A broker checks that the two wordings meet without a gap on the one scenario that involves both: a vulnerability in the firm's product that leads to a breach at the client.

Phishing reports fell 21% to about 4,800 in 2025 from 6,100 in 2024, which CSA notes may reflect under-reporting rather than fewer attempts. Why clients demand professional indemnity for IT companies in Singapore

Most Singapore IT firms first buy PI because a contract requires it. Enterprise, bank and government procurement terms specify a PI limit and ask for the certificate before the purchase order issues.

The benchmark the market copies comes from the regulated professions. Lawyers must hold professional indemnity cover before the Law Society issues a practising certificate, under Section 75A of the Legal Profession Act. The minimum is S$1 million per claim for sole proprietorships and partnerships and S$2 million per claim for law corporations and limited liability law partnerships, costs and expenses included. Procurement teams apply the same logic to technology vendors: a limit per claim, costs inclusive or exclusive, maintained for a period after delivery.

The second reason is the Personal Data Protection Act. Since 1 October 2022 the PDPC can impose a financial penalty of up to S$1 million or 10% of annual turnover in Singapore, whichever is higher, on organisations with Singapore turnover above S$10 million. A client fined after a breach caused by its vendor's product looks to the vendor. The vendor's PI, not the client's cyber policy, is where that claim lands.

The third reason is the firm's own balance sheet. A S$600,000 claim with S$150,000 of defence costs ends a 20-person software house without insurance. With PI, the firm pays the excess and continues.

What limit, and on what basis

The limit is set per claim, with an aggregate for the policy year. Three choices shape the premium.

  • Per claim limit. S$1 million is the floor most contracts specify; S$2 million to S$5 million is common for firms serving banks and government. Larger limits are bought where one contract carries most of the revenue.
  • Costs inclusive or in addition. Costs inclusive means defence costs erode the limit. Costs in addition means they are paid on top. Contracts increasingly specify costs in addition.
  • Excess. A higher excess lowers premium. The excess should be an amount the firm can pay 2 or 3 times in a bad year.

Worked example. The figures are an example.

A software firm with S$6 million revenue holds a S$2 million costs-inclusive PI policy with a S$25,000 excess. A client claims S$1.4 million for lost revenue after a failed ERP go-live. Defence costs reach S$350,000. The claim settles at S$1.1 million. Total S$1.45 million is within the limit; the firm pays S$25,000. Had the policy been S$1 million costs inclusive, the firm would have faced S$450,000 uninsured. The limit should be set against the largest contract, not the average.

Rates hardened from 2019. Chubb's Singapore underwriting view records that PI pricing began to rise in 2019 and that underwriters narrowed appetite through 2021. Technology firms with large single contracts, fixed-price delivery or financial services clients pay more per dollar of limit than firms with many small contracts.

Claims-made and the retroactive date

PI is written on a claims-made basis. The policy that pays is the one in force when the claim is first made against the firm, not the one in force when the work was done. Two dates follow from this.

The retroactive date is the earliest date of work the policy will cover. Work done before it is excluded. A firm that switches insurer must carry its original retroactive date forward, or it loses cover for every project before the switch. A firm buying PI for the first time usually gets a retroactive date equal to inception, so its past projects are uninsured until a client agrees otherwise.

The second date is the end of the policy. When a technology firm stops trading, is acquired or lets its PI lapse, claims for past work have nowhere to go. Run-off cover, a policy that accepts claims for a fixed period after the firm stops, closes that gap. Acquirers ask for it in the sale agreement.

Notification matters as much. Most wordings require the insured to notify circumstances that may give rise to a claim as soon as it becomes aware of them. A client's email that a system is "not performing as agreed and we are reviewing our options" is a circumstance. Notify it in the policy year it arrives and the later claim attaches to that year. Wait, and the next insurer may exclude it as a known circumstance. The claims-made and retroactive date explainer covers the mechanics.

What the proposal form asks and why

Tech E&O underwriters price the firm's processes as much as its revenue. The proposal form asks for:

  • Revenue split by service line: software licensing, implementation, managed services, consulting, hardware resale.
  • Largest 5 contracts by value, with the client sector and the contract term.
  • Standard contract terms: whether liability is capped, at what multiple of fees, and whether consequential loss is excluded.
  • Development and release process: testing, staging, change control, rollback.
  • Subcontractor use and whether subcontractors carry their own PI.
  • Claims and circumstances in the last 5 years.

The contract terms question carries the most weight. A firm whose standard terms cap liability at 12 months' fees and exclude consequential loss presents a different risk from one that signs the client's paper uncapped. A broker reviews the firm's terms with the underwriter and uses them to support a lower rate or a higher limit. It also reviews the client contracts that override those terms, because the policy's contractual liability exclusion is read against them.

Frequently asked questions

Does PI cover a software bug that costs my client money?

Yes, if the client claims against the firm for the financial loss and the policy is a technology errors and omissions form that covers failure to perform. The policy pays damages and defence costs up to the limit. The cost of fixing the bug itself is usually excluded as a cost of redoing the work.

What is the difference between tech E&O and cyber insurance?

Tech E&O pays when the firm's work causes a client loss. Cyber pays when the firm's own systems are attacked or breached: forensics, restoration, notification, business interruption and regulatory costs. A breach at a client caused by a flaw in the firm's product can involve both policies.

What PI limit do enterprise clients usually demand?

S$1 million per claim is the common floor, matching the Law Society minimum for sole practitioners. Banks and government bodies often specify S$2 million to S$5 million, costs in addition. The limit should be set against the largest single contract, not average revenue.

Is PI claims-made and why does the retroactive date matter?

Yes. The policy in force when the claim is made pays, provided the work was done after the retroactive date. Losing the retroactive date on a change of insurer removes cover for all earlier projects. Run-off cover is needed when the firm stops trading or is sold.
