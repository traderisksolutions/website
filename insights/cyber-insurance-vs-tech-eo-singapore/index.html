Cyber insurance vs tech E&O in Singapore

By the TRS broking team13 Nov 20266 min read
The short answerCyber insurance pays for the costs and claims that follow a security failure: breach response, data restoration, business interruption and liability for leaked data. Technology errors and omissions (tech E&amp;O) pays when a client loses money because your software, system or service failed to perform. The trigger is different. Cyber responds to an attack or data event. Tech E&amp;O responds to a performance failure, with or without an attack.

The short difference

Cyber asks whether there was a security or privacy event. Tech E&O asks whether your work fell short of what you promised a client.

QuestionCyber insuranceTech E&O
What triggers itHacking, malware, ransomware, data breach, privacy eventError, omission, negligence or breach of contract in your service
Whose lossYour own costs first, then third-party claimsYour client's financial loss, claimed against you
Typical first-party coverForensics, notification, data restoration, lost incomeNone
Typical third-party coverClaims and regulatory defence after a data eventClient claims for outage, defects, missed specifications
Policy basisUsually claims-madeClaims-made, with a retroactive date
Who usually asks for itBoard, auditors, larger clientsClient contracts and procurement teams

A software house, managed service provider or SaaS business carries both exposures. A business that only uses technology, such as a distributor, mainly needs cyber.

What cyber insurance covers

A cyber policy has two halves. The first-party half pays your own costs after an event. The third-party half pays claims made against you because of it.

  • Incident response: forensic investigators, legal advice and crisis communications.
  • Notification: costs of informing affected individuals and the regulator.
  • Data and system restoration: rebuilding data and software damaged by an attack.
  • Business interruption: lost income while your own systems are down.
  • Cyber extortion: response costs for a ransomware demand, subject to the wording and the law.
  • Privacy liability: claims from people whose data was exposed, and defence of regulatory investigations.

Singapore law sets the stakes. Under the Personal Data Protection Act 2012, an organisation that assesses a breach as notifiable must notify the PDPC within 3 calendar days. Since 1 October 2022, the maximum financial penalty is 10% of annual Singapore turnover above S$10 million, otherwise S$1 million. Owners of critical information infrastructure also carry incident-reporting and audit duties under the Cybersecurity Act 2018.

The threat level is not static. CSA's Singapore Cyber Landscape 2024/2025 reported a rise in local ransomware and infected-infrastructure cases. The PDPA breach article covers the breach response in more detail.

What tech E&O covers

Tech E&O is professional indemnity written for technology work. It pays damages and defence costs when a client alleges your product or service caused them a financial loss.

Typical claims include:

  • A software release with a defect that stops a client's billing run for 2 days.
  • A migration project that runs late and breaches a delivery milestone.
  • A configuration error by a managed service provider that takes a client's site offline.
  • Software that fails to meet the specification in the statement of work.
  • A claim of infringement of a third party's intellectual property, where the wording includes it.

The policy defends the claim even when it has no merit. Defence costs on a contract dispute often run before any liability is decided.

Exposure lasts beyond the year the work was done. The Limitation Act 1959 gives 6 years to sue in contract or tort from when the cause of action accrues. Latent damage extends it. A tech E&O policy is usually claims-made, so it must be in force when the claim arrives, not only when the work was done. The retroactive date sets how far back past work is covered.

Tech E&O does not pay to fix your own code, refund your fees or cover your own lost income. Those are business costs, not liabilities to a client.

Where they overlap and where they leave gaps

The overlap sits in one scenario: a client's data is exposed through your system. The client's negligence claim falls under tech E&O. Individuals and the regulator fall under the privacy section of cyber.

ScenarioCyberTech E&O
Ransomware locks your own serversRespondsDoes not respond
Your code bug causes a client outage, no attackUsually does not respondResponds
Hacker uses your platform to reach a client's networkResponds to your costs; may respond to the client claimOften responds to the client claim
Client data leaked from your systemResponds to notification, regulator and privacy claimsMay respond to the client's negligence claim
You miss a delivery dateDoes not respondResponds if the client alleges negligence

Three gaps recur:

  1. Exclusions that point at each other. Cyber may exclude professional services; tech E&O may exclude data breaches. Bought separately, both can decline.
  2. Contractual liability. Both exclude liability accepted under contract beyond what the law imposes. An uncapped indemnity sits outside both.
  3. Different retroactive dates. A past event may fall inside one policy and outside the other.

Placing both with one insurer, or on a combined wording, removes the first gap. The second is fixed in the contract, not the policy.

Which one a business needs

Three scenarios show the split.

Scenario 1: a 40-person SaaS company. It hosts client data and its platform runs client operations. A breach creates notification duties under the PDPA. A bug creates client claims for lost revenue. It needs both, ideally on matching retroactive dates.

Scenario 2: an 8-person IT consultancy. It configures systems on client premises and holds little client data. Its main exposure is an error that costs a client money. It needs tech E&O first. A cyber policy still covers its own systems, email and invoices against fraud and ransomware.

Scenario 3: a 150-person distributor with an online ordering portal. It sells goods, not technology services. Its exposure is its own downtime and customer data. It needs cyber. Tech E&O adds little, because clients do not pay it for technology work.

The decision turns on two questions. Do clients pay you for technology work? Do you hold personal data or run systems others depend on? Two yes answers mean both covers.

Frequently asked questions

Which policy pays if my software bug causes a client outage?

Tech E&O, provided the client alleges a financial loss from your error. Cyber usually does not respond, because there was no security or privacy event. Check that the tech E&O wording does not exclude outages caused by your own systems.

Which policy pays if my client's data is leaked from my system?

Usually both, for different parts of the loss. Cyber pays incident response, notification and privacy claims from individuals and the regulator. Tech E&O may pay the client's own negligence claim against you, if the wording does not exclude data events.

Do I need both if I am a SaaS company?

In most cases, yes. A SaaS business sells a service clients rely on and holds their data. Each exposure needs its own trigger. Many insurers offer both on one combined policy, which avoids the two wordings excluding each other.

Does PI include cyber in Singapore wordings?

A standard PI wording does not include first-party cyber costs such as forensics, restoration or your own lost income. Some PI wordings exclude data breach claims entirely. Others add a limited cyber extension with a sub-limit. Read the definition of "claim" and the cyber exclusion before relying on it.
