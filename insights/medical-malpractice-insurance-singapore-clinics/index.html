Medical malpractice insurance for Singapore clinics and doctors

By the TRS broking team10 Nov 20266 min read
The short answerMedical malpractice insurance in Singapore is compulsory for every doctor who holds a practising certificate. Since January 2021, Section 36 of the Medical Registration Act requires cover approved by the Singapore Medical Council. Public-sector doctors are covered by a central programme. Private clinics and their doctors buy their own, and the clinic entity is usually not covered by the doctor's policy.

What the law requires of a doctor

The Medical Registration Act sets the rule. A doctor cannot be issued a practising certificate without adequate insurance or indemnity approved by the Medical Council. The requirement applies to every registered doctor, whether a locum, a sole practitioner or an employee.

Doctors and dentists employed by public healthcare institutions are covered by the MOH Holdings Medical Malpractice Insurance programme. It has run since 1 January 2017 and covers work done for or sanctioned by a public institution. It includes defence costs, representation at Medical Council inquiries and coroners' inquests, and run-off cover after the doctor leaves the public sector.

The programme covers work for the institution. It does not describe cover for private practice or paid locum work done elsewhere. A doctor who moves between sectors, or works in both, needs cover that follows the work rather than the employer.

Dentists face a parallel regime under the Singapore Dental Council. The structure of the decision is the same for a dental clinic as for a GP or specialist clinic.

Occurrence or claims-made: which basis suits a clinic?

Medical indemnity is sold on 2 bases. The difference decides who pays for a claim that surfaces years after treatment.

FeatureOccurrence basisClaims-made basis
TriggerTreatment date falls in the policy periodClaim is first made in the policy period
Cover after the policy lapsesYes, for treatment given while insuredNo, unless run-off cover is bought
Retroactive dateNot neededNeeded; earlier treatment is excluded
Changing providerStraightforwardPrior acts must be picked up by the new policy
Price patternHigher at inceptionLower early, rising as the claims tail builds

A claims-made policy is the common form for clinic professional indemnity bought from an insurer. The retroactive date must sit at or before the clinic's first day of trading. A gap of 1 day excludes every patient seen before it. The broker checks this date on every renewal and every change of insurer.

Occurrence cover removes the tail problem. A doctor who retires keeps cover for every patient treated while the policy ran. The trade-off is price and a smaller choice of providers in Singapore.

Mutual protection or an insurance policy?

Doctors in Singapore choose between 2 kinds of provider. A mutual medical defence organisation offers discretionary indemnity. An insurer offers a contract of insurance.

FeatureMutual defence organisationInsurance policy
Legal formMembership; indemnity is discretionaryContract; indemnity is a legal right within the wording
RegulationNot an insurance contractMAS-licensed insurer, policy wording governs
LimitOften no fixed limitFixed limit per claim and in aggregate
Clinic entityUsually not coveredCan be named as an insured
Non-clinical liabilitiesTypically excludedCan be added (public liability, cyber)

Discretion means the organisation decides whether to assist. In practice assistance is routine, but it is not enforceable. An insurance policy is enforceable, but only within its limit and exclusions. A clinic owner with employed doctors, locums and nurses often ends up with both: individual cover for each doctor and an entity policy for the business.

Why the clinic entity needs its own policy

A patient who sues names the doctor and the clinic. The clinic is liable for its employees, for the system of care, for sterilisation, for record-keeping and for front-desk errors. A doctor's individual indemnity responds to the doctor's own clinical acts. It does not extend to the company.

Clinic professional indemnity, sometimes called medical establishment indemnity, covers:

  • Vicarious liability for employed doctors, nurses and allied health staff.
  • Clinical acts by nurses and therapists working under supervision.
  • Errors in appointments, test results and referrals handled by staff.
  • Defence costs for Ministry of Health and Medical Council inquiries directed at the licensee.

Nurses and support staff are not covered by a doctor's policy. They are covered by the entity policy if the wording names employees. The broker confirms that the employee definition includes part-time and agency staff.

Public liability for slips and falls in the waiting room is a separate section. Cyber cover for a patient-record breach is also separate. See cyber insurance and a PDPA breach for the data side.

Aesthetic clinics and higher-risk procedures

Aesthetic clinic insurance is priced differently from general practice. Underwriters ask for the procedure list: fillers, lasers, threads, liposuction, body contouring. Each carries a different claims pattern. Elective procedures attract more complaints because the patient paid for an outcome.

The largest malpractice judgment recorded in Singapore was S$5.3 million. It followed a liposuction death in 2015. That single figure explains why aesthetic limits are set higher than a GP clinic would carry. A S$1 million limit covers a typical complaint but not a catastrophic outcome.

Underwriters also ask about:

  • Whether procedures are done by the doctor or delegated to therapists.
  • Consent forms and cooling-off practice.
  • Marketing claims, which can turn a clinical complaint into a consumer dispute.
  • Overseas patients, since the territorial clause may exclude claims brought abroad.

What the claims data shows

The Medical Council referred 14, 10, 9, 11 and 11 cases to disciplinary tribunals in 2019 to 2023, 56 in total. The Dental Council referred 24 over the same 5 years. Tribunal cases are a small fraction of complaints, and civil claims are a small fraction again.

Health insurance premiums written in Singapore rose 7.4% to S$1.24 billion in 2025. Claims rose 6.4% to S$409.4 million. Those figures cover medical expense insurance, not malpractice, but they show the direction of treatment costs. Higher treatment costs raise the value of a malpractice award, because the award pays for corrective care.

Low frequency and rising severity is the pattern. It argues for a limit set against the worst realistic outcome rather than the cheapest policy on the market.

What moves the premium

No premium is quoted here. The broker's submission to an insurer covers 5 inputs that set the price:

  • Specialty and procedure mix. Surgery, obstetrics and aesthetics sit at the top of the scale.
  • Patient volume and revenue, which the rate is applied to.
  • Number of doctors, with locum days counted.
  • Claims and complaint history for 5 years, including Medical Council matters.
  • Limit and excess chosen, and whether the policy is occurrence or claims-made.

A clinic with 3 doctors, a stable procedure list and no complaints presents well. A clinic adding an aesthetics line should tell the broker before the first procedure, not at renewal. An undisclosed change in practice is a ground for an insurer to reduce or decline a claim.

Frequently asked questions

Does my employer's cover protect me if I also do locum work?

Usually not. Employer-arranged cover, including the public-sector programme, responds to work done for that employer. Locum sessions at another clinic are outside it. The locum needs individual cover that names the locum work, or the host clinic's policy must extend to visiting doctors.

Occurrence or claims-made: which suits a clinic?

Claims-made suits a clinic that will keep buying cover and can manage its retroactive date. Occurrence suits a doctor who wants cover to outlast the policy, for example before retirement. Many clinics run claims-made for the entity and leave each doctor to choose.

Does the clinic entity need its own policy in addition to each doctor's?

Yes, where the clinic employs staff or operates as a company. A doctor's indemnity does not cover the company's vicarious liability or its systems. The entity policy fills that gap and covers nurses and allied staff.

Are nurses and support staff covered?

Not under a doctor's individual policy. They are covered under a clinic entity policy that defines employees to include them. Agency and part-time staff should be named in that definition.
