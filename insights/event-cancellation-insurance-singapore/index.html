Event cancellation insurance in Singapore: cover, triggers, cost

By the TRS broking team10 Nov 20266 min read
The short answerEvent cancellation insurance in Singapore reimburses an organiser's irrecoverable costs, and optionally lost revenue, when an event is cancelled, abandoned, postponed or relocated for a cause beyond its control. It is a separate policy from event liability, which pays for injury or damage at the event. Adverse weather, non-appearance and communicable disease are each covered only if named in the policy.

Cancellation cover or event liability?

Organisers buy 2 policies for the same event. They answer different questions.

FeatureEvent cancellationEvent liability
What is paidThe organiser's own sunk costs and, if selected, net profitThird-party injury or property damage claims
TriggerEvent cannot proceed for a cause beyond the organiser's controlNegligence causing harm to attendees, venue or public
Who requires itSponsors, lenders, the organiser's own boardVenues, as a condition of hire or licence
Typical limit basisTotal budgeted cost or gross revenuePer occurrence, S$1 million upward
Timing to bindWeeks or months before the event, before costs are committedAny time before doors open

Venues in Singapore require public liability as standard practice before confirming a booking. Cancellation cover is not required by anyone but protects the organiser's money. One Singapore insurer sells an events liability package with a cancellation and abandonment option. Its published option carries a S$10,000 limit any one occurrence and in the aggregate, for S$109 indoor or S$163.50 outdoor including GST. That limit suits a company dinner or a community event. A conference with S$800,000 of committed cost needs a stand-alone cancellation policy.

Why the Singapore events market matters

Tourism receipts reached a record S$29.8 billion in 2024. MICE business travellers rose from 800,000 in 2019 to 1.2 million in 2024. Concerts, conferences and exhibitions are being booked up to 2 years ahead, with deposits paid at signing.

Committed cost grows every month before the event. Venue deposits, production, talent fees, marketing and travel are paid out before a ticket is sold. A cancellation 2 weeks before the date loses most of that money. The insurance exists because the organiser's exposure peaks when its ability to recover is lowest.

Larger events also carry more parties with a claim on the organiser. Sponsors want their fee back, exhibitors want their stand fees back, and ticket holders are owed refunds under the terms of sale. Cancellation cover pays the organiser's own loss; the refund obligations are met from that payment.

What triggers a cancellation claim

The base wording responds to causes beyond the organiser's control that make the event impossible or impractical. The usual list:

  • Venue damage or unavailability after fire, flood or structural failure.
  • Failure of power, water or transport serving the venue.
  • Death, injury or illness of a person on whom the event depends, where non-appearance is included.
  • A government order or licence withdrawal not caused by the organiser's own breach.
  • National mourning.
  • Adverse weather, for outdoor events, where the extension is bought.

The cause must be beyond the organiser's control. Poor ticket sales, a sponsor withdrawing, a budget shortfall or a change of mind are never covered. Breach of contract by the organiser is excluded. The policy insures against bad luck, not a bad plan.

Non-appearance, adverse weather and haze

Three extensions carry most of the price and most of the argument at claim time.

Non-appearance cover pays when a named performer, speaker or team cannot attend through death, accident, illness or travel disruption. Each person is scheduled by name with a value attached. A headliner with a publicised health history may be excluded or rated higher. The organiser's contract with the performer decides how much is at stake: a non-refundable fee, or a fee payable only on performance.

Adverse weather cover pays when weather makes an outdoor event impossible or unsafe. Underwriters define the trigger: rainfall in millimetres over a stated period, wind speed, lightning within a radius. A reading from a named station decides the claim. Rain that reduces attendance but does not stop the event is not a trigger unless the policy says so.

Haze is weather in common speech but not in most wordings. A PSI reading above a threshold can be named as a trigger, with a stated station and period. If it is not named, haze is not covered. Singapore outdoor events in the third quarter should ask for it by name.

Communicable disease after 2020

Communicable disease is excluded from standard event cancellation wordings written since 2020. Cover for an outbreak, a quarantine order or a government ban on gatherings is available only as a specific buy-back, with its own limit and premium, and from a narrower set of insurers.

Organisers should read 3 clauses together: the communicable disease exclusion, the government order trigger and the force majeure wording in the venue contract. A venue that keeps the deposit after a government ban leaves the organiser with a loss that only a buy-back pays. A venue that refunds leaves less to insure.

Where a buy-back is not available or not affordable, the contract is the only tool. Postponement rights, staged deposits and refund triggers negotiated into the venue and supplier agreements reduce the uninsured loss.

How limits and premium are set

The sum insured is set 1 of 2 ways. Costs only: the total irrecoverable expenditure budgeted for the event. Costs plus profit: the gross revenue expected, so that lost margin is paid as well as sunk cost. The second is dearer and requires a revenue forecast the underwriter accepts.

Worked example. A 2-day conference budgets S$600,000 of cost: venue S$180,000, production S$220,000, speakers S$120,000, marketing S$80,000. Ticket revenue is forecast at S$900,000. Insured on a costs-only basis the limit is S$600,000. Insured for gross revenue it is S$900,000. If the venue is flooded 10 days out and S$450,000 has been paid and cannot be recovered, the costs-only policy pays S$450,000 less the excess. The example is arithmetic, not a quotation.

No premium is quoted here. The rate moves with:

  • Indoor or outdoor, and the season.
  • Extensions bought: non-appearance, weather, communicable disease, terrorism.
  • Lead time at binding, with longer lead time rated lower.
  • Refund terms in the venue and supplier contracts.
  • The organiser's track record of delivered events.
  • Excess, usually a percentage of the sum insured.

Binding cover in time

Cancellation cover must be bound before costs are committed and before any known threat exists. Insurers decline cover for a weather system already forecast, a performer already unwell or a venue dispute already under way. A policy bound 2 months out at the first deposit covers the whole build-up. One bound the week before covers little and costs more.

The broker collects the budget, the contracts and the run sheet, places the cover, and issues the certificate the venue and sponsors ask for. Changes after binding, such as a new headliner or a move outdoors, must be declared and endorsed before the event. Event liability sits alongside it. Frequently asked questions

Does cancellation cover include haze or adverse weather?

Only if the extension is bought and the trigger is defined. Weather cover names rainfall, wind or lightning thresholds measured at a stated station. Haze is covered only where a PSI threshold is written in. Without those definitions neither is a trigger.

Is non-appearance of a headline act covered?

Only where non-appearance cover is added and the performer is scheduled by name. It pays when the performer cannot attend through death, accident, illness or travel failure. A performer who chooses not to appear is a contract dispute, not an insured event.

How far before the event must I bind cover?

As soon as the first irrecoverable cost is committed, typically 2 to 12 months out. Cover excludes any circumstance known at binding, so a forecast storm or an unwell performer cannot be insured after the fact. Earlier binding also rates lower.

Is communicable disease excluded?

Yes, in standard wordings since 2020. Cover for outbreaks, quarantine and gathering bans is available only as a priced buy-back with its own limit, from a smaller group of insurers. The venue contract's force majeure clause decides how much of that loss sits with the organiser.
