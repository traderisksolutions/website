D&O vs professional indemnity insurance in Singapore

By the TRS broking team13 Nov 20265 min read
The short answerDirectors and officers (D&amp;O) insurance pays claims against individual directors and officers for wrongful acts in managing the company. Professional indemnity (PI) insurance pays claims against the firm by clients who lost money through an error in its professional service. D&amp;O protects the people who run the business. PI protects the business for the work it sells. One rarely replaces the other.

The short difference

The test is who is sued and for what. A claim about a management decision points to D&O. A claim about client work points to PI.

Who is protectedDirectors and officers personally, and the company where it indemnifies themThe firm and its staff

Both are written on a claims-made basis. The policy in force when the claim is made responds, not the policy in force when the act happened.

What D&O covers

D&O pays defence costs, settlements and damages when a director or officer is sued for a wrongful act in that role. Most policies have three parts.

  • Side A: pays the director directly when the company cannot or will not indemnify, for example in insolvency.
  • Side B: reimburses the company when it indemnifies a director.
  • Side C: covers the company itself, usually only for securities claims in listed companies.

The legal basis sits in the Companies Act 1967. Section 157 requires a director to act honestly and use reasonable diligence in the discharge of the role. A breach exposes the director personally to civil and criminal liability.

Section 172 voids any provision that exempts an officer from liability for negligence or breach of duty. The same section allows the company to buy and maintain insurance for its officers against that liability. That is why D&O exists: the company cannot contract the liability away, but it can insure it.

Typical claimants are shareholders, liquidators of an insolvent company, creditors, regulators and employees. The D&O guide for private companies covers private-company claims in more detail.

What professional indemnity covers

PI pays damages and defence costs when a client alleges the firm's professional work caused a financial loss. Typical insureds are accountants, consultants, engineers, architects, IT firms and brokers.

Typical claims include:

  • An accounting firm misses a filing deadline and the client is penalised.
  • A consultant's market study overstates demand and the client loses its investment.
  • An engineer's design error requires remedial works.
  • A report relied on by a lender contains a material error.

Some professions must hold PI by regulation. An accounting LLP registered with ACRA must hold PI cover equal to the highest of three amounts. These are S$1 million, S$500,000 per corporate practitioner, or 2.5 times gross income in the last financial year, capped at S$50 million.

The exposure runs for years. The Limitation Act 1959 gives 6 years to sue in contract or tort from when the cause of action accrues. Latent damage extends it. A claim about work done in 2024 can arrive in 2030. The PI policy in force in 2030 must cover it, and its retroactive date must reach back to 2024.

Where they overlap and where they leave gaps

The overlap is narrow. A client can sue a director personally as well as the firm for negligent advice. PI usually covers employees and directors for claims about professional services. D&O usually excludes claims arising from professional services. The client claim therefore sits in PI.

The gaps are more common:

  1. Professional services exclusion in D&O. A consultancy director sued by a client for bad advice finds no cover in D&O. PI must respond.
  2. Management claims excluded from PI. A shareholder suing a director over a failed acquisition finds no cover in PI. D&O must respond.
  3. Insolvency. A liquidator suing directors for trading while insolvent is a D&O claim. PI does not respond.
  4. Regulatory investigations. D&O often pays legal costs in investigations of directors. PI cover for regulatory proceedings varies by wording.
  5. Mismatched retroactive dates. A firm that changes insurer and loses its PI retroactive date leaves past work uninsured.

A firm should map its likely claimants against both wordings. Each claimant should land in one policy.

Which one a business needs

Three scenarios show the split.

Scenario 1: a 15-person engineering consultancy, founder-owned. Its main exposure is a client claim for a design error. It needs PI. D&O matters less while the founders own all the shares and there are no outside lenders.

Scenario 2: a 200-person distributor with a private equity investor. It sells goods, not advice, so PI adds little. The investor, lenders and a future liquidator are the likely claimants against directors. It needs D&O.

Scenario 3: a 60-person accounting firm structured as an LLP. ACRA requires PI for the firm. The partners also run a business with employees, landlords and lenders. It needs both: PI by regulation, D&O for management claims.

The rule of thumb: a firm that sells advice or design needs PI. A firm with outside shareholders, lenders or independent directors needs D&O. Many need both.

Frequently asked questions

Does D&O pay for a client's negligence claim against the firm?

Usually not. Most D&O wordings exclude claims arising from professional services the company provides. That claim belongs in the firm's PI policy.

Does PI protect a director sued personally?

PI usually covers directors and employees when a client sues them personally over professional work. It does not cover a director sued for management decisions, such as breach of duty to shareholders. That claim needs D&O.

Which one do investors ask for?

Investors usually ask for D&O, especially when they take a board seat. Their nominee director wants protection for decisions made on the board. Clients and procurement teams ask for PI.

Can one policy do both for a small consultancy?

Some insurers offer a management liability package with D&O and PI sections in one policy. Each section keeps its own trigger, limit and exclusions. Check whether the limit is shared between sections, because one large claim can exhaust it.
