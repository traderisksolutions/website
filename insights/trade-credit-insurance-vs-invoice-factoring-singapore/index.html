Trade credit insurance vs invoice factoring for Singapore SMEs

By the TRS broking team10 Nov 20266 min read
The short answerTrade credit insurance vs factoring is a choice between protection and liquidity. Trade credit insurance pays an insured percentage of an invoice when the buyer becomes insolvent or defaults for a set period. Invoice factoring advances cash against the invoice before the buyer pays. Insurance replaces a loss. Factoring brings forward collection. Many Singapore businesses use both, with insurance sitting behind the financing.

What trade credit insurance does

Trade credit insurance covers accounts receivable against non-payment. The policy responds to two events. Insolvency means the buyer enters liquidation, judicial management or a scheme of arrangement. Protracted default means the buyer has not paid by a fixed number of days past the due date, with no formal insolvency. The policy wording sets that number of days.

The insurer sets a credit limit on each buyer. Sales within the limit are insured. Sales above it are not. The policy also sets an insured percentage, so the business keeps a share of every loss. A whole turnover policy covers all credit customers. A single buyer policy covers one named buyer.

The policy does not cover goods damaged or lost in transit. That is marine cargo insurance, which responds to the goods rather than the debt. It does not cover disputes either. If the buyer refuses to pay because the goods were faulty, the insurer waits for the dispute to be resolved.

The reason to buy it is concentration. One buyer at 30% of sales is a risk the balance sheet cannot absorb. Compulsory liquidations in Singapore rose from 307 in 2024 to 392 in 2025, the two highest counts in the SingStat series since 2006.

What invoice factoring does

Factoring is receivables financing. A business sells or assigns its invoices to a factor, usually a bank or finance company. The factor advances a percentage of the invoice value at once. When the buyer pays, the factor releases the balance less fees and interest.

Factoring comes in two forms. With recourse, the business must repay the advance if the buyer never pays. The credit risk stays with the business. Without recourse, the factor takes the credit risk on approved buyers, and prices that risk into the fee. Non-recourse factors often buy their own trade credit insurance to carry it.

The factor charges two things. A service fee on invoice value covers administration and collection. Interest runs on the advance for the days it is outstanding. The buyer is usually notified and pays the factor directly. Confidential facilities exist but cost more.

Factoring suits a business with a cash gap between delivery and payment. A contractor paid at 60 days but paying workers weekly is the standard case. The facility grows with sales, unlike a fixed overdraft.

The table shows why the two are not substitutes. A business with slow payers but solvent buyers needs factoring. A business with prompt payers and one fragile buyer needs insurance. A business with both problems often uses an insured receivables facility.

Worked example: a S$200,000 invoice

The figures below are an example. Rates and fees vary by insurer, factor and buyer.

A Singapore supplier issues a S$200,000 invoice on 60-day terms to a buyer with a credit limit of S$250,000 under a whole turnover policy. The insured percentage is 85%.

Insurance outcome if the buyer is wound up:

  • Loss within the credit limit: S$200,000.
  • Insurer pays 85%: S$170,000, after the claims waiting period.
  • Supplier absorbs S$30,000.

Factoring outcome if the supplier instead factors the invoice with recourse at an 80% advance:

  • Cash on day 1: S$160,000.
  • Buyer pays on day 60: factor releases S$40,000 less fees and interest.
  • Buyer is wound up instead: supplier repays the S$160,000 advance. The loss is the full S$200,000.

Combined outcome with insurance assigned to the factor:

  • Cash on day 1: S$160,000.
  • Buyer is wound up: insurer pays S$170,000 to the factor, which settles the advance and remits S$10,000 less charges.
  • Supplier's loss is S$30,000 plus fees.

The combination is why banks ask for a credit insurance policy before offering non-recourse terms.

How government schemes fit in Singapore

Two EnterpriseSG programmes sit near this decision. Neither replaces either product.

The Enterprise Financing Scheme Trade Loan supports working capital for trade. EnterpriseSG shares 50% of the default risk with the participating bank, or 70% for young enterprises and challenged markets. The maximum is S$50 million per borrower group across all EFS facilities, with repayment within 1 year. The borrower must have at least 30% local shareholding and group turnover of no more than S$500 million. Enhancements take effect on 1 April 2026.

The Trade Credit Insurance Scheme supported 50% of the minimum premium on policies from Singapore-registered credit insurers, up to S$100,000 per company. The latest extension the broker can trace ended on 31 March 2021. Confirm current status with EnterpriseSG before budgeting for it.

Risk-share on a trade loan does not protect the borrower. The borrower repays 100% of the loan whether or not its buyer pays. Only insurance moves the buyer's default off the business.

Which one does a Singapore SME need?

Start with the question the balance sheet asks.

  • Receivables are concentrated in 1 to 3 buyers: insurance first. One failure is the whole year's profit.
  • Receivables are spread but slow: factoring first. The cost of waiting is interest, not loss.
  • Sales are growing faster than the overdraft: factoring, with insurance if the new buyers are unrated.
  • Exporting to markets with transfer risk: insurance with a political risk extension.
  • Selling to government bodies or listed groups on 30 days: often neither.

The broker's work is on the insurance side. It builds the buyer list, requests credit limits from 2 or 3 insurers and compares the limits granted, not only the rate. A policy with a low rate and small limits on the two buyers that matter is poor value. The trade credit page lists what the submission requires.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between insolvency and protracted default?

Insolvency is a formal event: liquidation, judicial management or a scheme of arrangement. Protracted default is non-payment past a number of days after due date fixed in the policy, with no formal event. Both trigger a claim. Protracted default claims pay later because the waiting period runs first.

Can I insure only one large buyer?

Yes, through a single buyer policy. The insurer sets a credit limit on that buyer and charges a higher rate per dollar than on whole turnover cover. Insurers decline single buyer requests more often, because the applicant knows the buyer better than the insurer does.

Does factoring protect me if the buyer never pays?

Only without recourse. With recourse, the business repays the advance when the buyer defaults, so the loss is the full invoice plus fees. Non-recourse factors take the default risk on approved buyers and price it into the fee, often backed by their own credit insurance.

Who needs trade credit insurance in Singapore?

Businesses selling on open account with concentration in a few buyers, exporters selling into markets with transfer risk, and businesses whose bank asks for insured receivables before lending. Compulsory liquidations reached 392 in 2025, up from 307 in 2024.
