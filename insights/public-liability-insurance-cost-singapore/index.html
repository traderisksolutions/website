Public liability insurance cost in Singapore: what sets the premium

By the TRS broking team13 Nov 20265 min read
The short answerPublic liability insurance in Singapore is usually bought inside a package for small premises. One insurer's published office package includes S$500,000 of cover for S$185.30 a year, with each extra S$500,000 costing S$54.50. Businesses with site work, high footfall or contract limits above S$5 million are rated individually, on activity, turnover, limit and claims history.

Key takeaways

  • Public liability is not required by statute in Singapore. Leases and contracts set the limit most businesses buy.
  • Published package rates add S$54.50 a year for each S$500,000 of extra limit, up to S$5 million.
  • Activity drives the rate more than size. Work on third-party premises costs more than office work.
  • Public liability does not cover employees. Injury to staff falls under WICA.

What public liability insurance covers

Public liability insurance pays sums the business is legally liable to pay a third party. It covers accidental bodily injury and damage to property. It also pays legal costs of defending a covered claim.

A visitor slipping on a wet shop floor is the standard claim. So is a contractor's worker damaging a client's ceiling while installing equipment.

Three exposures sit outside it:

  • Employees. Injury to staff is covered by WICA insurance and employer's liability, not public liability.
  • Products after they leave the premises. Product liability covers injury from goods sold or supplied. The two are compared in public liability vs product liability.
  • Professional advice. Financial loss from negligent advice falls under professional indemnity.

Package policies sometimes add a narrow extension for food served on the premises. Liberty's OfficeCare and ShopCare include a food and beverage extension of S$50,000 per loss and in aggregate.

The limit is "any one accident, unlimited any one period" on these packages. Each accident has its own limit, with no annual cap.

What sets the premium

Seven factors set a public liability premium for a business rated outside a package.

Business activity

Activity is the first rating factor. An office receiving few visitors rates low. Renovation, cleaning, security and event work on third-party premises rate higher.

Turnover or wages

Insurers apply the rate to annual turnover, or to wages for labour-intensive trades. A business that doubles its turnover pays about double, unless the rate changes.

Limit of indemnity

The limit is the most the insurer pays for one accident. Each additional block of limit costs less than the first.

Work away from the premises

Work at client sites, in public areas or at height increases exposure. Insurers ask for the share of turnover earned off-site.

Contract terms

Contracts may require the policy to name the client as an additional insured. Some require the insurer to waive subrogation against the client. Each extension can add premium.

Excess

A property damage excess removes small claims from the policy and the record. Bodily injury claims usually carry no excess.

Claims history

Insurers ask for recent claims records. Repeated slip-and-fall claims at the same premises signal a housekeeping problem and raise the rate. A written incident log and repair records help the broker present the risk.

What contracts ask for

Contracts, not statute, set the limit most businesses carry. Public-sector construction works use the Public Sector Standard Conditions of Contract (PSSCOC), published by the Building and Construction Authority. PSSCOC contracts require third-party liability insurance.

Private leases and service contracts set their own limits. The broker reads the insurance clause and checks four points:

  1. The limit per accident, and whether an annual aggregate applies.
  2. Whether the client or landlord must be named on the policy.
  3. Whether the contract requires a waiver of subrogation.
  4. How many days' notice of cancellation the client must receive.

A limit chosen without reading the contract is often too low or carries the wrong wording.

The broker also checks how long the cover must stay in force. Some contracts require liability cover for a period after the work ends. A policy cancelled at contract completion would breach that term.

Worked examples

Example 1. A 15-person design consultancy in a leased office. Limit: S$1 million, set by the lease. It fits a published office package. Few visitors and no site work keep the rate at the package level.

Example 2. A 40-person cleaning contractor serving malls and offices. Limit: S$2 million, set by client contracts. Off-site work on wet floors in public areas is the main driver. Clients' additional insured requirements add wording. Damage to client property in the contractor's care needs its own extension.

Example 3. A 120-person mechanical and electrical contractor on public projects. Limit: S$5 million under PSSCOC tenders. Work at height and in occupied buildings drives the rate. Claims history across 3 years of projects sets the final terms.

Frequently asked questions

What limit do landlords and main contractors usually ask for?

The lease or contract states it, and it varies by building and project. Liberty's published office and shop packages offer S$500,000 or S$1 million as base limits. Higher limits are set by the contract and rated on the business's own data.

Does public liability insurance cover my employees?

No. Injury to employees is covered by WICA insurance and employer's liability. Public liability covers third parties such as visitors, customers and the public.

Why does a food business pay more than an office?

Food businesses have more visitors, wet floors, hot surfaces and food-borne illness exposure. Each raises the chance of an injury claim. Packages often limit food claims to a sub-limit, such as Liberty's S$50,000 food and beverage extension.

Can I pay monthly?

Some bank-distributed packages quote a monthly price, and the insurer collects by instalment. Policies placed through a broker are usually annual, with the premium due at inception. Instalment terms depend on the insurer and premium size.
