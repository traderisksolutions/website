Marine cargo insurance cost in Singapore

By the TRS broking team13 Nov 20265 min read
The short answerMarine cargo insurance is priced as a rate applied to the insured value of the goods. The insured value is usually the CIF or CIP value plus 10%. The rate depends on the goods, the route, the mode of transport, the packing, the clauses chosen and the shipper's claims record. War and strikes risks are rated separately and can change at short notice.

Key takeaways

  • Premium equals the insured value multiplied by the rate. The insured value is normally invoice value plus freight and insurance, plus 10%.
  • The goods and their packing move the rate more than the distance travelled.
  • War risk is a separate rate set per voyage area. Hull war rates for Strait of Hormuz transits rose from 0.10%–0.25% to 3.5%–7.5% per transit in 2026.
  • A carrier's legal liability is capped, so the cargo policy carries most of the loss.

Factors that move the price

Insured value

The sum insured sets the base. Under Incoterms 2020, a seller on CIF or CIP terms must insure for at least 110% of the contract price. Buyers on other terms usually adopt the same basis to cover freight, duty and lost margin.

Goods

Electronics, pharmaceuticals and branded consumer goods attract theft. Glass, ceramics and machinery attract breakage. Frozen and chilled goods need temperature cover. Each raises the rate.

Clauses

The Institute Cargo Clauses (A) cover all risks, subject to exclusions. Clauses (B) and (C) cover named perils only. Wider cover costs more. The Institute Cargo Clauses guide sets out the differences.

Mode and transhipment

Air cargo spends fewer days in transit and is handled less. Sea cargo spends weeks in transit and may be transhipped. Each handling point adds damage and theft risk.

Route and destination

Destinations with port congestion, theft or conflict exposure rate higher. Inland legs on poor roads add risk.

Packing

Goods in sealed containers suffer less than goods shipped loose. Poor packing is a standard exclusion. Insurers check packing on fragile goods.

Volume and policy type

An annual open cover spreads the insurer's cost over many shipments. It usually carries a lower rate than buying cover shipment by shipment.

Claims record

Insurers read 3 to 5 years of losses. Repeated theft or water damage claims raise the rate and the excess.

War risk in 2026

War and strikes risks sit outside the standard clauses. They are added by separate Institute War and Strikes Clauses, with their own rate.

In a written parliamentary reply on 4 August 2026, the Minister for Transport addressed rising war-risk premiums from the US-Iran conflict. Industry estimates as of July 2026 put war-risk cover for Strait of Hormuz transits at 3.5% to 7.5% of hull and machinery value per transit. Before the conflict the range was 0.10% to 0.25%. These figures are for ship hulls, not cargo. The reply gives no figure for cargo war rates.

The same reply noted that Asia to North Europe container rates rose about 120% from pre-conflict levels. Higher freight raises the CIF value, and the cargo premium rises with it. The Minister said shipments to and from Singapore were less affected than Middle East trades.

Insurers can review or cancel war rates at short notice. A shipper with Middle East trades should confirm the war rate before each shipment.

Underinsurance and the carrier's limit

Two rules make an accurate sum insured matter.

First, underinsurance. Section 81 of the Marine Insurance Act 1906 sets it out. An assured insured below the insurable value is deemed their own insurer for the uninsured balance. Cargo insured at 80% of its value recovers 80% of each loss.

Second, the carrier's limit. Under the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act, which applies the Hague-Visby Rules, a sea carrier's liability is capped. The cap is 666.67 SDR per package or 2 SDR per kilogram, whichever is higher. On a container of high-value electronics the cap may cover a small part of the loss. The cargo policy carries the balance.

Volumes are large. MPA reported container throughput of 41.12 million TEUs in 2024. A Singapore importer or exporter shipping weekly builds up exposure fast, which is why most move to open cover.

Worked examples

Example 1: electronics exporter on open cover. A 50-person distributor ships about 200 containers a year of consumer electronics to Asia and Europe. Each shipment is insured at CIF plus 10% under Institute Cargo Clauses (A). Its price is moved by theft exposure, transhipment points and its claims record. Annual volume lowers the rate per shipment.

Example 2: machinery importer, single shipment. A manufacturer imports one S$2 million production line by sea from Europe. The sum insured is CIF plus 10%, with duty added. Its price is moved by breakage risk, heavy-lift handling, packing and the single-shipment basis. Insurers may ask for a pre-shipment survey.

Example 3: food trader to the Gulf. A 20-person trader ships frozen seafood by sea to the Middle East. The sum insured is CIF plus 10%. Its price is moved by temperature risk, the reefer breakdown condition and war risk on the route. The war rate is set per voyage and may change between quotation and shipment.

Frequently asked questions

Is cargo premium charged on CIF plus 10%?

Usually, yes. Incoterms 2020 require a seller on CIF or CIP terms to insure for at least 110% of the contract price. The rate is applied to that insured value.

Why is air freight cheaper to insure than sea?

Air cargo spends less time in transit and passes through fewer handling points. Exposure to water damage, theft and delay is lower. Rates also depend on the goods, so this is not true for every shipment.

When does a war-risk surcharge apply?

When war and strikes cover is added to the policy, and the voyage touches an area insurers list as high risk. The insurer sets an additional rate per voyage. It can change or be withdrawn at short notice.

Open cover or per shipment: which costs less?

For a business shipping regularly, open cover usually costs less per shipment. It also avoids gaps when a shipment is not declared in time. A business with 1 or 2 shipments a year may find single-shipment cover adequate.
