Money insurance in Singapore: cash in transit and on premises

By the TRS broking team10 Nov 20266 min read
The short answerMoney in transit insurance pays for cash, cheques and vouchers lost by theft or robbery while being carried between the business and the bank. It is one of 4 money situations a commercial money section covers: in transit, on the premises during business hours, in a locked safe after hours, and in locked drawers after hours. Published Singapore F&amp;B package limits run from S$500 to S$5,000 per situation before top-up.

Money in transit insurance and the 3 other money situations

A money section in a Singapore package policy divides cash by where it is and when. Each situation has its own limit and its own conditions.

SituationWhat is coveredTypical condition
In transitCash carried by the insured or an employee to or from the bank, or to a cash-collection pointCarried by a named or adult employee, not left unattended
On premises in business hoursCash in tills, at the counter or in the back office while the business is openPremises occupied
In a locked safe after hoursCash left overnight in a safe or strongroomSafe locked, keys removed from the premises or held by a named person
In locked drawers after hoursSmall floats left in locked drawers or cash registers overnightDrawers locked; lowest limit of the four

"Money" is defined in the policy and usually includes cash, bank notes, cheques, postal orders, stamps and gift vouchers. It does not include stock, and it does not include electronic funds; a fraudulent transfer is a crime or cyber claim, not a money claim.

Most package policies also include a personal accident assault extension. It pays a fixed benefit if an employee is injured or killed during a robbery while carrying or guarding money, separate from any work injury compensation claim.

Limits in Singapore SME packages

Insurers publish the money limits in their SME packages. Liberty's CafeCare plan for F&B operators sets the following, with a top-up available at S$6 per S$500 of additional cover.

SituationRestaurant Plan ARestaurant Plan BFood Stall Plan AFood Stall Plan BTop-up maximum
In transitS$3,000S$5,000S$1,000S$2,000S$10,000
On premises, business hoursS$3,000S$5,000S$1,000S$2,000S$10,000
Locked safe after hoursS$3,000S$5,000S$1,000S$2,000S$10,000
Locked drawers after hoursS$1,000S$2,000S$500S$1,000S$3,000

Two things follow from the table. The limits are per situation, not per year, so a restaurant on Plan B that is robbed on the way to the bank recovers up to S$5,000 for that event. And the drawer limit is deliberately low, because insurers want cash in a safe overnight, not in the till.

Example only. A restaurant banks S$9,000 of weekend takings on Monday morning. On Plan B it is insured for S$5,000 in transit. Buying S$4,000 of top-up at S$6 per S$500 costs S$48 a year and brings the transit limit to S$9,000. The alternative is to bank twice over the weekend and keep each run under S$5,000.

Exclusions that decide claims

Money claims are refused on conditions more often than on the definition of money. The common exclusions are:

  • Unattended vehicle. Cash left in a parked car, even locked, is excluded.
  • Unlocked safe or drawer. The after-hours limits apply only when the container was locked and the key was not left on the premises.
  • Employee dishonesty. Theft by an employee is excluded from the money section and belongs under fidelity guarantee.
  • Unexplained shortage. A till that is S$300 short at close with no evidence of theft is an accounting loss, not an insured one.
  • Carrying limits. Some wordings require 2 adults above a stated sum, often S$5,000 or S$10,000.
  • Late discovery. Losses must usually be reported within a set period, often 7 days, and to the police.

A police report is a condition of almost every money claim. For a robbery, the report also starts the criminal process: robbery under Section 392 of the Penal Code 1871 carries imprisonment of 2 to 10 years and caning. For the insurer, the report is the evidence that a theft occurred rather than a shortage.

Money insurance versus fidelity guarantee

The 2 covers are confused because both involve cash going missing. They respond to different people.

FeatureMoney insuranceFidelity guarantee
Who takes the moneyA third party: robber, burglar, thiefAn employee
WhereIn transit, on premises, in safe or drawerAnywhere the employee has access, including bank accounts
How discoveredAt once, usually with a police reportOften months later, at audit or reconciliation
Limit basisPer situation, per eventPer employee or per event, with an aggregate
Proof requiredEvidence of theft by force or stealthEvidence of dishonesty and the amount, often an audit trail

Fidelity guarantee insurance covers loss of money or stock caused by the dishonesty of employees, including theft, embezzlement and fraud. It is written for a named list of positions or for all employees, with a limit per person. A cashier who skims S$200 a night for a year has taken S$73,000; the money section pays nothing, and fidelity guarantee pays up to its limit once the loss is proved.

A cash-heavy business should hold both. The money section handles the external risk; fidelity guarantee handles the internal one.

Setting limits for F&B and retail

The right money limits come from the cash cycle, not from the package default. The broker asks 4 questions:

  1. What is the peak cash on the premises at any moment, including the float, the day's takings and any uncollected weekend cash? That sets the business-hours and safe limits.
  2. How often is cash banked, and what is the largest single run? That sets the transit limit.
  3. Is there a safe, is it rated, and who holds the key? An unrated safe or a key left on site lowers the insurer's appetite for the after-hours limit.
  4. Is a cash-in-transit contractor used? If so, the contractor's own cover responds in transit and the business needs cover only until handover.

Example only. A hawker stall takes S$1,800 a day in cash and banks daily. Food Stall Plan A, with S$1,000 in transit and on premises, is short by S$800 on a normal day and more on a public holiday. Plan B at S$2,000, or Plan A with a S$1,000 top-up, matches the cycle. A retail chain with 6 outlets banking S$15,000 a week each is outside the package range and needs a stand-alone money policy with a transit limit of S$15,000 or more per outlet.

Reducing cash is the other control. Every outlet that moves 60% of sales to cards or QR payment cuts the insured exposure and the premium with it. Insurers ask for the cash-to-card split on the proposal form for exactly that reason.

Frequently asked questions

Is cash stolen from a staff member on the way to the bank covered?

Yes, under the in-transit situation, up to its limit, provided the conditions were met. The employee must have been carrying the cash as instructed, not have left it unattended, and the loss must be reported to the police and the insurer within the policy's time limits. Any 2-person carrying requirement above a stated sum must also have been followed.

What if the safe was not locked?

The after-hours safe limit applies only to money in a locked safe. Cash taken from a safe that was left open, or whose key was left on the premises, falls outside that situation. The insurer may consider the lower locked-drawer limit if the money was elsewhere, but more often the claim fails.

Does money insurance cover an employee who pockets takings?

No. Theft by an employee is excluded from the money section. It is covered by fidelity guarantee insurance, which pays for loss caused by the dishonesty of employees up to a limit per person, once the dishonesty and the amount are proved.

How much money cover does a hawker stall need?

Enough to cover the largest amount of cash held or carried at any one time. A stall banking S$1,800 a day needs a transit and premises limit of at least S$2,000, which is the Food Stall Plan B level in one published package. A stall that banks less often, or holds weekend takings, needs more.
